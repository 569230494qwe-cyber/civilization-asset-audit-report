## Chapter 31: The Dimension Marx Left Unfinished — Gender as Base Collateral

The last chapter's account was settled only halfway: there is a gap in his theory. This chapter begins from that gap.

**1. What Marx Accomplished**

Before writing this chapter, settle accounts with Marx first.

Among the classical political economists, he is the only one who put "the human being" at the center. Smith saw "man" as a self-interested agent in the market; Ricardo saw "man" as a factor of production; Marx saw "man" as exploited labor.

His theory of surplus value stripped off capitalism's innermost secret. He saw the accumulation of capital, saw the source of profit, saw how the poverty of the workers is systematically manufactured.

He did not stop in the economic domain; he also took apart the superstructure. He saw that religion is "the opium of the people," that law is "the will of the ruling class," that morality is "the ruling ideas." These judgments still read sharp today.

He saw "man," saw "capital," saw "history."

But he did not see "gender."

**2. The Gap He Left**

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, Marx wrote:

"The relation of man to woman is the most natural relation of human being to human being."

He wrote the first half of this sentence. The second half, he never finished writing.

In *Das Kapital*, Marx analyzed "labor power" as a given fact. He pressed the question: what determines the value of labor power? Answer: the value of the means of subsistence necessary to maintain the worker and his family.

"And his family" — four characters, and everything is glossed over.

He did not press further: what happens inside this "family"? Who maintains it? How much is the labor of maintaining it worth? Is that labor counted into the formula of "the value of labor power"?

The answer is: it is not counted. It is free.

But Marx did not pursue it further.

**3. He Could Not Pursue It Any Further**

Not because he was not smart enough. The era did not let him pursue it.

In 1867, when Marx wrote *Das Kapital*, women in England had no property rights; married women were not regarded as independent legal persons; a wife was legally subordinate to her husband. Women going out to work was regarded as "indecent"; the ideal of the working class was one man earning, the wife at home.

In that era, to ask "how much is housework worth" would have been treated as a joke. The economics profession simply would not have regarded it as a question.

Marx himself was also a man of that era. He saw the workers, but his field of vision was the factory. He saw exploitation, but his yardstick was the wage. He saw capital, but his foothold was the relations of production.

He saw social reproduction, but he treated social reproduction as background.

**4. Gender as Base Collateral**

Smith, Ricardo, Marx — three generations of classical political economists share one common blind spot:

They all treated "the reproduction of labor power" as a natural process requiring no cost.

But the reproduction of labor power is not natural. It requires someone to cook, someone to wash, someone to give birth, someone to raise, someone to provide emotional support, someone to maintain the body and dignity of that worker so he can report to work again the next day.

That person is a woman.

Her labor is free. Her existence is collateral. She is not counted in "the value of labor power," not counted in "surplus value," not counted in "capital accumulation."

She is not on the ledger.

But she is the base collateral of all ledgers.

For the capitalist to extract the worker's surplus value, the premise is that the worker's labor power can be reproduced. For the worker to keep selling his labor power, the premise is that someone bears the housework and the caregiving for him. That person is not paid a wage, is not acknowledged as a "laborer," is not counted in any formula of economics.

This is what gender as base collateral means.

It is not a metaphor. It is the one reserve entry that classical political economy never wrote into the ledger, from beginning to end.

**5. Two Ledgers, One Machine**

Smith could not see the collateral beneath that hand.

Ricardo could not see the table beneath that table.

Marx saw capital's exploitation of labor, but not the male worker's appropriation of female labor.

The blind spots of these three generations, stacked together, constitute the complete boundary of classical political economy. They saw the logic of the market, the logic of capital, the logic of history.

It makes women willingly become the base collateral.

**6. After Marx**

After Marx, political economy split into two roads.

One was the academic school. They refined Marx's formulas, made the calculation of surplus value more detailed. But they still did not press the question: that cost of "reproducing labor power" — who exactly paid it?

One was the critical school. Starting from Marx, they criticized capital, criticized power, criticized ideology. But most of them still placed the gender question in the position of an "additional topic." They saw "class," saw "race," saw "colonialism," but "gender" was often only a chapter placed at the end.

Not until the second half of the twentieth century did feminist political economy begin systematically to pursue this question: how much, exactly, is unpaid household labor worth?

But even after that, mainstream economics still did not write "gender" into its underlying formulas.

Taking counterfeit notes for real money — the Chastity Coin, the Poverty Coin.

Taking the empty position for the full position — Wang Yangming.

Twenty-five hundred years ago, someone settled this general account once. The Buddha's own words: all beings are upside down. His itemized list has four entries; match them one by one:

Taking the impermanent as permanent. — Match: the Theocratic Narrative Bank, changing only its signboard for ten thousand years, never its accounts.

Taking suffering as happiness. — Match: the treadmill called striving, collection called for your own good, the wrapping paper of debt being love.

Taking no-self as self. — Match: the echo wall casting an "I"; the mouth opening and cutting out self and other.

Taking the impure as pure. — Match: the pure and the dirty minted into coins. The Chastity Coin collects "purity"; two characters collect "filth."

His language was religious, his audience monks. This book recalculated it in contracts, trending topics, medical records, and compressed files.

Two ledgers, twenty-five hundred years apart, the same figures.

This is called corroboration. Note the specification of corroboration: it is not his endorsement of this book — endorsement is embezzling the reserve, and this book does not do that. It is two independent audits reaching identical conclusions.

**3. No Thing Is Me; No Thing Is Not Me**

The ladder can now be laid out in full.

Sartre acknowledged the empty position. The empty position makes you run.

Wang Yangming declared the full position. The full position makes you believe.

The Buddha: no position.

The no-position does not tell you to do anything. It only says: the very concept of position is false. Empty position, full position — both are positions; and position is a word, a cut.

The two witnesses of the bee-and-wolf chapter have no position. Being precedes words.

This book does not teach people what to do. It has merely finished laying out the ladder: the fourth floor is the empty position, the fifth floor is the full position, and the rooftop has no position.
