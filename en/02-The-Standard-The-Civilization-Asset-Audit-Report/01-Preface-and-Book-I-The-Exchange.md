## Chapter 1: Language Is an Exchange of the Sense of Existence, Not a Container of Truth

The fundamental function of language is not description, but transaction.

Traditional philosophy's understanding of language stops at "language is the tool of thought, the picture that depicts the world." This assumption runs from Plato all the way to the early Wittgenstein. It presupposes a naive scene: first there is an objective world, then there is an "I" independent of language, and finally the "I" uses the neutral tool of language to describe the world.

Language has never been a tool of description. It is a medium of transaction.

The reason humans need language is not that we have thoughts that need expressing, but that language took away our ability to self-certify our existence.

Note the sequence: it is not that humans were innately deficient and then invented language to fill the gap; it is that humans first acquired language, and only then were made deficient by it.

A tiger does not need another tiger to tell it, "You are a tiger." The tiger is; it does not need to know that it is — it simply is. Humans were once the same. Before the birth of language, the human "is" and the tiger's "is" were no different: direct, whole, requiring no one's signature.

But the moment language was born, a transaction that changed the fate of humankind took place: humanity mortgaged the certificate of "confirming one's own existence" from its own hands into the mouths of others.

This was humanity's first mortgage, earlier than the Chastity Coin, earlier than all currency. What it bought was the entire dividend of cooperation, order, and civilization. But from that day on, humanity could never redeem that certificate — because the only tool for redeeming it is language, and language itself sits in the other party's hands.

And so a species innately whole became a species deficient by design.

When a primitive human first uttered the syllable "I," it was not to confirm itself before a mirror, but to send a signal to the others in the tribe: "I am here. Please see me. Please acknowledge my existence."

This "I" was, from the very beginning, a begging gesture — a check of the sense of existence, waiting to be cashed.

When we use the word "love," we are not describing an emotion; we are sending the other party a transaction request: "Please give me the feeling of being loved." Likewise, when the other party responds, "I love you too," she is not stating a fact; she is completing a transaction — with her language, she has converted the sense of existence for you.

When we use the word "morality," we are not stating an objective standard of good and evil; we are purchasing a sense of belonging. When I say "I observe morality," what I really mean is: "Please accept me as a member of the group; please acknowledge that I am a good person."

When we use the word "truth," we are not describing the world as it is; we are contending for an ultimate authority. When someone says "I hold the truth," what he really means is: "You should obey me."

This is the truth of language: it is a market that never closes, and every word is a currency waiting to be cashed. Its value depends not on whether it corresponds to objective fact, but on how widely it can be recognized as true — on how much of others' recognition and obedience it can be exchanged for.

The moment this market opened, language was no longer just the tool of transaction; it turned around and created the objects of transaction.

All the currencies that come later — the Chastity Coin, the Dignity Coin, the Poverty Coin, and that counterfeit note with a face value of ten thousand trillion — are all printed in this market.

## Chapter 2: Language Is Not a Mirror — It Creates Reality Rather Than Reflecting Fact

Traditional philosophy treats language as a mirror: the world is over there, and language reflects it over here; how faithful the reflection is constitutes the only standard for measuring language.

But you need only look around to discover an undeniable fact: the overwhelming majority of human civilization's reality was not "described" by language at all — it was created by it.

A piece of paper has no value in itself. But when an entire society acknowledges the word "money," that piece of paper can be exchanged for bread, for houses, for another person's whole life. The word created purchasing power.

A company does not exist in the physical world. The factory buildings are real, the machines are real, the employees are real — but the thing called "the company" cannot be touched or weighed. It exists only in the registration documents and in everyone's mutual acknowledgment. The word created organization.

A national border is a line on a map; on the ground there is nothing. The same grass grows on both banks of a river, the same wind blows across. But when everyone acknowledges that "this side is ours and that side is theirs," this invisible line can send young men with rifles to shoot at one another. The word created a boundary worth dying for.

This is the true power of language: when enough people use a vocabulary in common and recognize it as "real," that vocabulary turns around and shapes human cognition, behavior, and institutions.

It follows a fixed three-step process.

Step one, mutual recognition. Enough people say, "This is real."
Step two, institutionalization. Words become rules, rules become institutions, and institutions begin to issue certificates, collect taxes, and pass judgment.
Step three, internalization. The rules no longer need to be enforced from outside; they move into everyone's skull and become "conscience." You begin to supervise yourself, and you even believe it is your own voice.

When an entire civilization acknowledges the authority of the word "morality," a code of conduct can be internalized as the individual conscience. When everyone recognizes "love" as the foundation of marriage, countless people will exhaust their whole lives for this fictional concept.

So we do not live in reality. We live in a "web of meaning" woven by language.

This web is at once the foundation of our civilization and the prison of our souls. The "meaning" that human beings pursue all their lives is nothing but the currency circulating in the language market. It is not treasure; it is merely an IOU, endlessly re-circulated and steadily devalued.

Remember the sequence: it is not reality that produces words; it is words that produce reality.

Later we will see that the greatest fiction in human history was manufactured by exactly this process. When it was made, it was called "divine oracle"; later it was renamed "ritual propriety"; today it has changed its name again — it is called "risk control."

And this web obeys one more economic law — it inflates.

## Chapter 3: The Inflation of Language — A History of Devaluation from "Love" to "True Love"

Once you understand the monetary nature of language, you can explain a phenomenon that happens every day and that no one has ever stated plainly: the inflation of language.

When the word "love" was first coined, it was a currency with adequate credit. Saying "I love you" could be exchanged for the feeling of being loved. But as the frequency of use increased — as more and more people used the word to cover transaction, control, even violence — its credit began to fall. People discovered that when someone says "I love you," what he may really mean is "I want your money" or "I want your body."

And so the currency "love" was devalued.

To keep transactions going, humanity invented denominations of larger face value — "true love." It tried to declare itself different from the devalued "love": pure, unconditional, absolute. But the problem is that it still circulates in the same market. It too can be abused; it too can be used to package new transactions and new control. In the end even "true love" was devalued, and humanity invented the "soulmate" — a Zimbabwean note with a face value of ten billion.

This is not the fate of the word "love" alone. Leaf through the issuance history of human language, and every page shows the same curve of devaluation:

"Beauty" was once a compliment; now it is merely a gender. "Friend" once meant someone you'd trust with your life; now it is one line in a contacts list. Titles once marked real power and responsibility; now you walk into a foot-washing parlor and everyone is a "CEO." To signal sincerity we invented "very"; when "very" was no longer enough, "super"; when "super" was no longer enough, whatever this year's superlative slang happens to be — every era uses larger denominations to purchase the very same thing.

This is the inflation of language: to cover hypocrisy, we keep issuing words of ever larger face value. But every issuance overdraws the credit of the entire language system.

And the cost of inflation is never shared equally.

In monetary inflation, the ones hurt most are the poor holding cash. In language inflation, the ones hurt most are the honest — those who can only use the old small-denomination notes. A person who can only honestly say "you are very good" can exchange it for nothing in today's market. His words are genuine, but the denomination is too small; the market no longer honors them. He must learn to speak bigger words, swear more exaggerated oaths, use higher leverage, just to get back a single "I believe you."

And so sincerity itself becomes something that must be proven. And the process of proving sincerity, precisely, dilutes sincerity.

This is a cycle without brakes: hypocrisy causes devaluation, devaluation drives good money out, the exit of good money causes a more universal denomination race, and the denomination race causes deeper hypocrisy.

As for the engine of this inflation — why devaluation is unavoidable and irreparable — the answer hides in the craft by which currency is born. In Book II, we open that machine.

But for now, notice one exception: among all the inflating words, there is one that almost never devalues. Its purchasing power today is as stable as it was two thousand years ago; everyone honors it; no one dares refuse it.

It is called "should."

## Chapter 4: The Grammar of Morality Is the Syntax of Power

At the end of the last chapter we said there is one word that never devalues. Now let us inspect it — "should."

"You should be filial."
"You should work hard."
"You should have a sense of responsibility."

When someone says the word "should" to you, many people's first reaction is self-examination: "Am I not doing well enough?"

But look carefully at the grammatical structure of the sentence: the subject is "you," the predicate is "should," and the object is the behavior. This structure is asymmetrical by birth.

The person speaking automatically takes the position of "legislator," while you are stuffed into the position of "the obedient." This is not describing any objective standard of good and evil at all; it is merely an order, gently packaged. He has translated his naked will to power — "I want you to do this" — into the tender moral imperative "you should do this."

This is the secret of why "should" never devalues: it does not circulate on credit; it circulates on position. The one who says "I want" bears the cost of conflict; the one who says "you should" does not. Power always chooses the currency with the lowest cost.

But the cruelest thing about morality is not that it commands you — it is that, in the end, it makes you command yourself.

At the beginning, the parents' "you should be obedient" and the teacher's "you should study" are external voices. If you don't listen, you might be beaten, you might be made to stand in the corner. But as time passes, these voices are spoken hundreds of times, tens of thousands of times, and are recognized by everyone as "a matter of course."

At that point, the external voices disappear. They become an echo wall inside your brain.

If you don't go home for the New Year, it is no longer your parents scolding you — it is an indescribable guilt arising inside you. If you are unmarried at thirty, it is no longer society mocking you — it is you trying yourself, late at night. The voice in your head that collects debts for the power structure has been given a very sacred name by humankind: "conscience."

Conscience is not an innate moral instinct. Conscience is the shackle you put on yourself after being domesticated.

Why obey such moral commands? Because morality is an extremely cost-effective transaction.

When you obey "you should be filial," what you hand over is obedience, and what you exchange it for is your parents' approval, your family's acceptance, and the sense-of-existence currency of being a "good son." If you do not obey, society immediately strips you of your sense of existence, labels you "unfilial," a "traitor," and liquidates you publicly in the language market.

So morality is not a commandment from heaven; it is a social transaction contract. With the sentence "you should," it makes you willingly hand over your freedom in exchange for that recognition from others on which your survival depends.

This is the ultimate truth of morality: a monetary system that exercises the most thorough power with the gentlest grammar.

But this contract has a fatal loophole that no one has ever examined —

The contract is full of what "you" should do. But this "you" who signs and seals it — this "you" who is required to obey, required to feel guilty, required to redeem for a lifetime —

who, exactly, is it?

## Chapter 5: The So-Called "Self" Is Nothing but a Fictional Narrative, Repeatedly Spoken, Tending toward Stability

The loophole in the last chapter's contract has not yet been patched: that "you" who is required to obey, required to feel guilty, required to redeem for a lifetime — who is it?

You will certainly say: it's me, of course. I have my name, my personality, my memories, my principles.

Fine. Then let us take them apart one by one.

We usually assume that every person has an internal, unique, essential "self." This self exists prior to language, and then uses language to express itself.

But the truth is thoroughly inverted: it is not that the self exists first and language follows; it is that language exists first, and the self follows.

Your "self" is pieced together from fictional symbols downloaded from outside.

The first fictional symbol is the name. Your parents gave you a name and then addressed you by it, over and over. An infant does not initially know who "himself" is; but he hears the name, hears his parents interact with him through it, and gradually learns: when this name is heard, respond. This process is the downloading and installing of the first linguistic fragment of the "self." The name is not you, but it becomes the first linguistic label you use to identify "yourself."

Then comes identity. You grow up; your parents say "you're a good child," your teachers say "you're a smart student," society says "you're a promising young person." These external voices repeatedly instill in you descriptions of "who you are." You accept these descriptions and begin to define yourself by them. You say to yourself: "I am a good student." You no longer need external voices to tell you — you yourself have become the repeater of that voice.

Then comes desire. You think it is you who wants that car, that piece of clothing, that lifestyle — but in fact it is only a "need" implanted in your heart by advertising, by social media, by the expectations of the people around you. You mistake external voices for your own. You have never had a desire truly your own — your desires are merely the echoes left in your body by the language environment you inhabit.

And then there is suffering. You are heartbroken, unemployed, defeated. You think you are enduring some real, substantial pain — but the structure of that pain deserves a whole chapter of dissection, and we will dissect it in the next one.

For now, answer a larger question: if the self is merely a collection of external linguistic fragments, why do we feel it is so real, so unified, so stable?

Because language has the "echo wall" effect.

When all external voices are telling similar stories — parents saying "you're a good child," teachers saying "you're a good student," friends saying "you're a kind person," society saying "you're a success" — these voices form an echo wall inside your inner world. They reverberate repeatedly, corroborate one another, and finally form a narrative pattern tending toward stability.

This stable narrative is what we call "personality" or the "self." It is not stable because you truly possess some essence; it is stable because the linguistic signals fed in are consistent enough that the narrative produced can remain self-consistent. When the signals contradict — when someone long recognized as a "good child" is suddenly accused of being a "bad person" — he falls into enormous confusion and pain. This is not because his essence has been denied; it is because his narrative has taken a shock, and a crack has appeared in his echo wall.

What happens when the echo wall shatters completely? That is Book V's business; when the time comes, you will see that it has a medical name.

For now, remember this chapter's ultimate corollary: that "self" you spend your whole life protecting, maintaining, and proving is not some essence prior to language. It is merely a first-person narrative continuously produced by the language market.

An account book filled with other people's handwriting, bearing your name.

Note: what I am speaking of is this ledger-self — the one that is spoken, priced, and maintained. As for whether there is nothing at all beyond the ledger, I do not answer that now.

## Chapter 6: Suffering — The Irreconcilable Contradiction between First-Person Narrative and Third-Person Reality

The transactional nature of language, the power structure of morality, the fictitiousness of the self — all converge on a single point, delivering a final verdict on "suffering," the most fundamental existential experience of humankind.

Human beings suffer not because their desires go unsatisfied, but because the stories they tell about themselves break irreconcilably against the cold reality of the external world.

Since ancient times, human explanations of suffering have revolved around "desire." Buddhism says suffering stems from greed, hatred, and delusion; Schopenhauer said life is a pendulum between the pain of unfulfilled desire and the boredom of desire fulfilled. All these explanations presuppose the same premise: suffering exists because "what I want was not obtained."

Then what, exactly, is the thing that desire grabs at and refuses to release?

It is narrative. The object of desire is never an object or a person; it is the story about "me" woven around that object or that person. The essence of suffering is not the frustration of desire — it is the margin call of a narrative.

When a person falls out of love, his pain does not come merely from losing the other person's company. His pain comes, rather, from the fact that the core proposition in his first-person narrative — "I am someone worthy of being loved" — is thoroughly falsified by the third-person fact of the other party's departure. He has not only lost a person; he has lost the story on which he relied to understand and define himself. His pain is the bankruptcy of a personal mythology.

Likewise, when a person is judged by society to be a "loser," his pain comes not merely from material scarcity, but from the unbridgeable rift between his inner narrative "I should succeed" and the external world's verdict "you have failed." He has been abandoned by the story he believed in with all his heart.

With the language-transaction model, we can dissect this bankruptcy process more precisely.

Every person is in the language market, using the narrative they tell about "who I am" to exchange for others' recognition, respect, and love. That recognition, respect, and love is the sense-of-existence currency on which they survive.

"I am someone worthy of being loved" — this is the core bond he has issued. With this narrative as collateral, he exchanges with the world for the experience of being loved. When someone responds to his narrative and loves him, his bond is redeemed. His sense-of-existence account gains a balance.

But what is betrayal? Betrayal is the other party declaring, by her behavior, that his bond is waste paper. She says: "I no longer love you." That sentence amounts to a credit downgrade of his core narrative in the existence market. His bond collapses instantly; the sense-of-existence currency on which his life depended turns to nothing overnight.

Mental suffering is, in essence, this credit collapse in the existence-trading market. It is not the loss of an emotion; it is an economic bankruptcy — the instant circuit-breaker of an entire system of meaning.

This is suffering. Not the frustration of desire, but the bankruptcy of narrative. Not failing to obtain, but being proven unworthy of obtaining.

**Redemption Clause (1)**

This chapter's theory carries a check, and the redemption date is written here.

My prediction is: the intensity of mental suffering is proportional to the narrative position held, and is unrelated to the event itself.

With the same unemployment, the person heavily positioned in "I should succeed" collapses, while the person with an empty position feels nothing. With the same heartbreak, the person who staked the entire self on "we will be together forever" goes bankrupt, while the person with a diversified portfolio merely takes a loss.

This prediction has a testable twin: psychotherapy. The core operation of modern psychotherapy is precisely narrative rewriting — the facts do not change, the story changes, and the suffering disappears. If my theory is right, therapy should work; if suffering is unrelated to narrative, rewriting the story should be entirely useless.

So the redemption clause is: if facts prove that the degree of suffering is determined solely by the event itself and is unrelated to the narrative the individual holds — that all people suffer equally from the same event, whatever their stories — this chapter's theory is void on the spot.

If an individual's bond can suffer a margin call, what about a bond issued by a civilization? If the public debt held by an entire generation were declared in default, all at once?

## Chapter 7: The Inflation of Meaning — The Great Outbreak of Nihilism

The last chapter left a question: if an individual's bond can suffer a margin call, what about the bonds issued by a civilization?

In this chapter, we apply the entire language-transaction theory to the macro-civilizational level, and dissect the grandest word that governs all the others — "meaning."

The question to be answered is: why, in our era, does nihilism spread like a plague?

Return to the core insight of Chapter 1: language is an exchange of the sense of existence. In this market, every individual spends every day using his behavior, speech, and identity to exchange for others' recognition. And "meaning" is the highest-denomination public debt in this market, issued and guaranteed by civilization as a whole.

When a person asks, "What is the meaning of my life?", he is not pursuing a philosophical proposition; he is conducting an anxious audit of value. His real psychological activity is: "Can my existence be exchanged for enough sense of existence to make me feel secure? Can what I do be acknowledged, remembered, and legitimized by some being grander and more authoritative than myself — God, the nation, history?"

"Meaning" is the ultimate letter of recognition issued by an external authority, certifying that your existence is not in vain. It is not a discovery; it is a credit. It is not an objectively existing fact; it is a sense-of-existence bond issued to you by civilization, guaranteed by specific words.

Human society needs this public debt to keep running. To make individuals willingly participate in the social division of labor, accept sacrifice, and endure pain, civilization must use grand narratives to legitimize these behaviors. Ancient warriors were willing to go to their deaths because the word "glory" had extremely high credit in the language market; modern people are willing to work 996 because the word "success" is recognized by society as the hard currency convertible into the sense of existence.

However, it is precisely the kind of hypocrisy we dissected in Book I — using sacred words to package power and transaction — that has caused a credit crisis across the entire meaning market. When people discover that "glory" is nothing but a fig leaf for a general's promotion and fortune, that "success" is nothing but the sugar coating on the extraction of surplus value, the credit of these words inevitably heads for collapse.

To keep the system running, civilization must keep manufacturing ever more gorgeous, ever more absolute words to serve as collateral for "meaning." This is a desperate credit expansion. From "love" to "true love" to "soulmate," from "dedication" to "mission" to "changing humanity" — every upgrade of vocabulary uses a larger-denomination currency to fill the void left by the collapse of the old one.

This is "the inflation of meaning."

But inflation cannot go on forever. When all of a civilization's ultimate collateral — God, nation, ideals, historical laws — goes bankrupt in succession due to its own logical contradictions and practical failures, the entire meaning market circuit-breaks in an instant.

Nihilism erupts from this. It is not a theory Nietzsche invented in his study; it is the systemic rout felt in the depths of every soul holding those bonds when a civilization's meaning-debt is declared wholly in default.

People stand on the ruins of meaning, look around, and find that all the words are waste paper, all the narratives are frauds, and all the authorities are beggars draped in imperial robes. They do not "hold the opinion" that life is meaningless — they have suddenly discovered that the entire language system that once guaranteed "life has meaning" has completely collapsed.

The fleeting new memes young people play with are precisely the temporary currencies spontaneously used by a new generation on the ruins after the old meaning-currency collapsed — circulating only within small circles. They do not need profound significance; they need only, at this moment, within a limited crowd, to complete one brief exchange of the sense of existence. This in itself is the most thorough mockery and abandonment of the old language system.

This is the true moment of nihilism's outbreak.

It is not the end point; it is the starting point — the blank gap in which the old currency has entirely collapsed and the new currency has not yet been discovered. Humanity stands in this gap, on the ruins of the old order, waiting for the arrival of a new trading rule.

But before that, according to the theory I myself laid down, there is one account that must first be settled.

I must declare that this book is also a currency, and that it is composed of two kinds of coin. The mechanisms — suffering, the reserve, the debt chain — are checks, with redemption dates written on them: if wrong, they die; you are welcome to redeem them. The metaphors — the four banks, the four-headed snake, the narrative central bank — are lenses, not scales. A lens is not responsible for being verified; it is only responsible for letting you see what you could not see before. If you saw through it, its task is complete; if you saw nothing through it, it will not argue with you.

After ferrying people across, the Buddha said: those who understand that my teaching is like a raft should let go even of the Dharma — how much more so of what is not the Dharma. The raft that crossed the river must be burned once across. The person who keeps carrying the raft on his back after crossing has made the raft into a new shackle. This book is my raft.
Use it, see through all of this, then burn it.

End of Book I.
